Part 1

How the model works today

1.1

What it predicts

For every active US customer, the model estimates how likely they are to buy — in each product category — within the next 30 days. It ranks the categories per customer and writes each customer's top three (with a confidence score and a High / Medium / Low band) onto their Klaviyo profile, so campaigns can target and personalize off them.

It is a per-customer, per-category model: one prediction for each (customer, category) pair — which is what lets it recommend which categories to show someone.

1.2

The data it learns from

The model reads Angara's curated reporting warehouse (Redshift) — not raw clickstream. For the US model it draws on history from January 2024 onward, resolved to a customer identity and anchored to the US timezone:

  • On-site behavior — visits, product views, cart-adds, checkouts and orders, by category / device / channel (~2.0M rows).
  • Email engagement — Klaviyo opens and clicks (~16.9M rows).
  • Subscription & tenure — subscriber status and how long they've been a customer (~286K rows).
  • Identity resolution — anonymous visitors are stitched to a known email, so history follows the customer.
1.3

The method

History is turned into a large set of labeled examples. At many points in the past — weekly snapshots — for each customer and category, we look back 30 days to summarize behavior, and forward 30 days to record what happened:

Look back 30 daysvisits, product views, cart-adds, email opens — the recent signals (features)
Snapshot
Next 30 daysdid they buy this category? → the answer to learn (label)

Repeated for every customer × category at each weekly snapshot across ~2.5 years → millions of labeled examples.

A gradient-boosted decision-tree model (LightGBM today) learns from those examples — building many small trees, each correcting the last, to learn patterns like "a recent cart-add on Rings plus two visits → high chance of buying Rings." Because only ~1% of examples are buyers, the model is weighted so it takes the rare buyers seriously.

1.4

Training — how the data is split

The model is trained and tested on a time-based split, never a random one. That matters: a random split would let the model "see the future" (leakage) and report accuracy it wouldn't achieve live. Splitting by time makes the test a fair rehearsal of predicting a real, unseen future.

A worked example, using the current model's as-of date of July 5, 2026 — the full history (Jan 2024 → Jul 2026) is taken as 131 weekly snapshots, then divided:

Snapshots after Jun 5 are still scored for delivery, but excluded from judging because their outcome isn't final yet.
  • Class imbalance: buyers are ~1% of examples, so the model is weighted (≈ 91:1) to treat them as important rather than predicting "nobody buys."
  • Deployed model: after the honest test, the final model is refit on all mature data (train + validation) so it learns from the most recent complete month too — while the reported metrics stay based on the untouched test.
1.5

Validation — reading the charts & the fit

Every run produces the same diagnostics. Here's what each one means and why it matters.

AUC — the headline accuracy

The model scores AUC ≈ 0.86. AUC answers: given one real buyer and one non-buyer, how often does the model rank the buyer higher? 0.86 = ~86% of the time, against a coin-flip of 0.50 and a real-world base buy-rate of only ~1%. It's the single best summary of ranking quality — and ranking is exactly what targeting needs.

Precision, recall & the confusion matrix

At a chosen score threshold, precision = of those we flag as buyers, how many really buy; recall = of the real buyers, how many we catch; F1 balances the two, and the confusion matrix shows the raw hits and misses. These are a reporting lens — the threshold is a dial we set for the report, not something that changes the scores sent to Klaviyo.

Is the model well-fit? (over- vs under-fitting)

The key health check is the gap between training accuracy and validation accuracy:

  • Small gap (here ~0.02): the model generalizes — it isn't memorizing the training data (that would show as high training, low validation = over-fitting).
  • Early stopping: the model keeps adding trees only while validation accuracy improves, and stops when it plateaus — an automatic guard against over-fitting.
  • Not under-fit either: 0.86 vs a 0.50 baseline (and strong decile lift) shows it's genuinely learned the signal, not too simple.
1.6

The features

The model reads a compact set of signals per (customer, category):

days_since_last_activerecency of any activity in the category
product_views / cart_adds / checkouts / ordersfunnel depth in the last 30 days
visitssessions in the last 30 days
channels / deviceshow & where they arrive
propensity_categorythe category itself (see below)
email_opens / email_clicksengagement in the last 30 days
subscriber_channel / is_subscribed / days_since_subscribedsubscription & tenure

Recency dominates (~50% of the model). That's a strength for engagement, but it's also the root of the issue Part 2 addresses. Why the category is itself a feature: including it lets the model learn each category's own base rate and behavior, and — because we score every (customer, category) pair — it's what enables a ranked top-3 per person. The result is genuinely personalized: across 234K customers the top pick spans 313 different categories, with no single category above ~6.5% — not a popularity default.

1.7

The output

Each customer gets a top-3 list written to Klaviyo — per rank: score, band, stone, jewelry type, category — plus prop_model_version, prop_scored_at (data as-of date), and a stable test_bucket (1–100) for clean A/B holdouts. Scores are grouped into bands (thresholds High ≥ 0.61, Medium ≥ 0.21). Current US distribution by each customer's top category:

LowMediumHigh
Low · 156,691 Medium · 71,136 High · 6,662

Total ≈ 234,480 profiles synced (9 unrecoverable emails excluded). Bands are how the email team segments audiences by intent.

Part 2

What's changing — the next model (v3)

2.1

Why we're changing it

Validation surfaced a real weakness: customers who had just purchased were being scored too high. Digging in, the cause was the feature mix — the model leans ~50% on days_since_last_active, which counts a purchase as "activity" identically to a browse. So a recent buyer looks maximally "active" and floats into High — even though, in our own data, a recent purchase is a negative signal for buying again within 30 days:

Group (mature data)Buy again in 30 days
Did not purchase recently2.24%
Purchased recently (any category)1.32%
Baseline (everyone)2.11%

So the fix isn't a bug patch — it's giving the model a way to tell a recent buyer apart from a recent browser, and using an algorithm that leans less on raw recency. That's v3.

2.2

Change 1 — CatBoost engine

We switch the learning engine from LightGBM to CatBoost (same family — boosted trees — so the pipeline is unchanged). CatBoost handles our text/categorical fields (channel, subscriber channel, device, category) far better via its "ordered target" encoding, and is more resistant to over-fitting. The effect on our data:

  • Higher accuracy — held-out AUC 0.864 → 0.877.
  • Less recency-dominance — it pulls real signal from the category/channel fields, so it doesn't over-rely on "recently active," which directly eases the recent-buyer problem.
2.3

Change 2 — a purchase-recency feature

We add days_since_last_purchase — how long since the customer last bought that category (over their full history, not just the 30-day window). This gives the model the dedicated signal it was missing, so it can separate "recently browsed" (a positive signal) from "recently bought" (a near-term negative) instead of lumping both into one recency number. It needs no new warehouse data — it's derived from orders we already load.

2.4

Combined result

Together, the two changes lift accuracy and clean up the top band. Validated on a like-for-like rebuild at the same as-of date (identical customers, identical delivery — only the model differs):

Net

v3 is more accurate and puts fewer just-purchased customers in the priority band — a strict improvement, with the same audience coverage. It reduces the recent-buyer contamination rather than eliminating it (those customers are still mid-intent, not low); a hard suppression rule remains available if the team ever wants them fully excluded.

2.5

Operational changes

  • Model version → v3. v3 scores are on a different scale than v2 and are not directly comparable (as with v1→v2). Bands land in nearly the same place, so no re-segmentation is needed — High simply becomes a bit smaller and purer.
  • Switchable engine. The engine is a one-line setting; LightGBM is retained as a fallback.
  • New audit log. Every sync now also writes to a Redshift table (propensity.klaviyo_sync_log) — the exact payload plus send date, model version and dry-run flag — so any future question about "what did we send, to whom, when" is answerable.
  • Unchanged: the data sources, identity resolution, feature windows, and the Klaviyo delivery mechanics all stay the same.
Part 3

Roadmap

  • Product-attribute affinity features — a customer's revealed preference for metal, style, collection, stone origin (and price band): dimensions the category alone doesn't capture, so they add fresh signal to the model and unlock richer creative personalization.
  • Forward validation — measure the v3 cohorts on purchases after scoring (once the warehouse advances), to confirm the gains on truly unseen data.
  • Percentile-based bands — define High/Med/Low by score percentile so band sizes stay stable automatically across future retrains.

Reference

Glossary

Propensity
The estimated probability a customer buys a given category in the next 30 days.
AUC
Area under the ROC curve — how well the model ranks buyers above non-buyers (0.5 = random, 1.0 = perfect). Our headline metric.
Feature importance (gain)
The share of the model's accuracy attributable to each feature.
Band
High / Medium / Low grouping of the score, used to segment audiences.
Snapshot
A point-in-time view of a customer used to build one training example.
Mature label
A snapshot whose 30-day outcome window has fully elapsed, so its answer is known.

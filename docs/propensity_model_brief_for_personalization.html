01 — What it predicts

A prediction of what each customer will buy next

For every known US customer, propensity estimates how likely they are to buy — not overall, but broken down by the attributes that describe a piece of jewelry.

It is a per‑customer, per‑value model: one prediction for each (customer, value) pair — (this customer, 14K White Gold), (this customer, Diamond), and so on. That per‑pair design is exactly what lets it rank a customer's values and hand back a genuine top‑3.

It runs across seven attributes, each predicted independently — category, stone, jewelry type, metal, price band, stone origin, and product gender. Every prediction carries a probability score (0–1) and a plain band (high / medium / low), over a 30‑day horizon. It's live today powering email personalization in Klaviyo; this brief is the current-state picture — the model, its outputs, and how it runs — documented for the personalization team.

02 — Inputs

The data it learns from

The model reads Angara's curated reporting warehouse (Redshift) — not raw clickstream — using history from January 2024 on, resolved to a customer identity and anchored to the US timezone:

  • On‑site behavior — visits, product views, cart‑adds, checkouts and orders, split by attribute value / device / channel (~2M rows per attribute).
  • Email engagement — Klaviyo opens and clicks (~17M rows).
  • Subscription & tenure — subscriber status and how long they've been a customer (~300K rows).
  • Identity resolution — anonymous visitors are stitched to a known email, so a customer's history follows them across sessions and devices. (Propensity therefore exists for identified customers — see the caveats.)

03 — Method

How the model learns

History is turned into a large set of labelled examples. At many points in the past — weekly snapshots — for each customer and value, we look back 30 days to summarize behavior, and forward 30 days to record what actually happened (did they buy that value?). Repeated for every customer × value at each weekly snapshot across ~2.5 years, that's millions of labelled examples.

A gradient-boosted decision-tree model — CatBoost — learns from them, building many small trees that each correct the last, to pick up patterns like "a recent cart-add on Rings plus two visits → high chance of buying Rings soon." It's learning from real outcomes, not rules we wrote by hand. Because only ~1% of examples are buyers, the model is weighted so it takes the rare buyers seriously rather than defaulting to "nobody buys."

One model per attribute. That entire method runs once for each of the seven attributes, so each score stands on its own grain. A customer's top metal is a genuine prediction about metal — not a by‑product of their top category. This is the heart of v4: every attribute carries its own score and band.

04 — Training

How the data is split

The model is trained and tested on a time-based split, never a random one. That matters: a random split would let the model "see the future" (leakage) and report an accuracy it would never hit live. Splitting by time makes the test a fair rehearsal of predicting a real, unseen future.

  • Older snapshots train; a later window tests. The test window uses only mature snapshots — those whose 30‑day outcome has fully elapsed — so the reported score is trustworthy. Snapshots too recent to have a final outcome are still scored for delivery, just excluded from judging.
  • Class imbalance handled. Buyers are ~1% of examples, so they're up-weighted — the model treats them as important instead of predicting no one buys.
  • Deployed model sees the freshest data. After the honest test, the final model is refit on all mature data so it learns from the most recent complete month too — while the reported metrics stay based on the untouched test.

05 — Trust

Validation & fit

AUC — the headline accuracy. Given one real buyer and one non-buyer, how often does the model rank the buyer higher? 0.5 is a coin flip, 1.0 is perfect. On held-out data, all seven attributes land between 0.85 and 0.89 — strong and consistent, especially against a real base buy-rate near 1%.

Is it well-fit?

The health check is the gap between training and validation accuracy. A small gap means the model generalizes — it isn't memorizing the training data (which would show as high training, low validation = over-fitting). Early stopping adds trees only while validation keeps improving and halts at the plateau — an automatic guard. And at 0.85–0.89 against a 0.50 baseline, it's clearly not under-fit either: it has genuinely learned the signal.

Precision, recall and the confusion matrix are a reporting lens at a chosen score threshold — a dial we set for reports, not something that changes the scores delivered downstream.

Score → band

The raw 0–1 score is bucketed so it's usable without reading probabilities:

A high band means the customer is genuinely likely to buy that value soon — a defensible line to gate a personalization treatment on.

06 — Signals

The features

Each prediction reads a compact set of signals per (customer, value): recency of activity, product views, cart-adds, checkouts and orders; email opens and clicks; subscription and tenure; device and channel. One deliberate signal is days_since_last_purchase of that value — so the model can tell a recent browser (a positive signal) apart from a recent buyer (a near-term negative), instead of lumping both into one recency number.

Why the value is itself a feature. Feeding the value in lets the model learn each value's own base rate and behavior — and because we score every (customer, value) pair, that's what produces a ranked top‑3 per person. The result is genuinely personalized, not a popularity default: top picks spread across hundreds of distinct values, with no single one dominating.

07 — Output

What it produces — the customer profile

For each known customer, the model emits a compact profile: the top 3 categories, stones, jewelry types, and metals (ranked), plus the single top price band, stone origin, and product gender — each with its own score and band. It also ships grammar helpers (singular/plural forms) so copy reads cleanly.

08 — Operations

How it runs today

The model is batch-scored on a cadence — it is not a real-time, per-request service.

  • Daily refresh (9 PM PT). Re-scores customers active in the last 30 days (~20K a night) on the current models, and updates their profiles.
  • Weekly retrain (Mondays). Retrains all seven models on fresh data and re-scores the full ~250K base.
  • Delivery today. Profiles are pushed to Klaviyo for email personalization; every run is audited in Redshift.

In short: the signal already exists and refreshes itself on this schedule — daily for the active base, weekly for the full base.

09 — Caveats

Limits & caveats

  • Batch, not real-time. Scores update on a daily/weekly cadence — treat them as a preference layer, not a live per-session intent signal.
  • Identified customers only. Email-keyed, ~250K profiles; anonymous visitors have no propensity until they're resolved to an email.
  • Purchase propensity, not click intent. It predicts a 30‑day purchase of a value — a different thing from what someone is browsing this minute.
  • US only at present.
  • Daily covers the recently-active slice; the weekly retrain refreshes everyone.

Glossary

  • Score — probability (0–1) the customer buys that value within 30 days.
  • Band — score bucketed to high / medium / low for easy gating.
  • AUC — ranking accuracy on held-out data; 0.5 = chance, 1.0 = perfect.
  • Snapshot — a point-in-time view of a customer used to train and score.
  • Identified vs anonymous — propensity exists only for customers resolved to an email.
